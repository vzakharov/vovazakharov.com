# Delivery robots, sat on and kicked in Philadelphia

## Facts

Uber Eats showed its sidewalk delivery robots, made by Avride, to Philadelphia on 10 March 2026, and the service started that week. Each robot costs about $10,000, finds its way with cameras, ultrasonic sensors and lidar, travels at up to 5 mph, and is scheduled to work from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. in Center City, Chinatown and Old City. It has digital eyes that blink and wink.

On Saturday 14 March, the night of the Erin Express bar crawl before St. Patrick’s Day, one was filmed at 15th and Locust Streets while a man sat on top of it. Futurism describes the robot being “kicked, sat on, laughed at, and even humped by a crowd of onlookers”; 6abc describes the kick as “a playful kicking motion behind the bot.” Someone wrote “DESTROY ME PLZ” on its shell. The Inquirer places the writing in the same encounter; Futurism points to an Instagram video, which it presumes earlier, of the same robot already carrying it. The robot then rolled on to its delivery.

Around midnight on Saturday 28 March, eighteen days after the robots arrived, another robot was kicked over at Juniper and Locust Streets. The security cameras of a passing karaoke trolley recorded it, while the passengers sang “Mr. Brightside.” The robots are not meant to be out after 10 p.m.; Uber did not answer The Inquirer on why this one was. Fox News, citing WPVI, reports that it was kicked more than once, went over on the second kick, and was set back on its wheels by the people who had kicked it.

Philadelphia police are not investigating either incident. Under a Pennsylvania law of 2020 the robots are “personal delivery devices,” with the same standing on a sidewalk as a pedestrian. None of the sources names anyone who did it.

By May, The Inquirer counted the robots “kicked, blocked, toppled, run over, mounted, graffitied,” and found residents helping ones that had got stuck.

## Statements

A street photographer who filmed the robot that night, in the caption Futurism quotes: “Honestly thought it was done for… One wanted to throw it, another sat on it. another person wrote destroy me on it… Then it calmly rolled on its merry way.”

Avride, to Fox News: “When delivery robots are introduced in a new area, it’s quite common to see heightened curiosity from people around them. Some may try to ‘test’ how the robot reacts — for example, by stepping in front of it or attempting to interact with it directly.” And: “These few cases of vandalism in Philadelphia did not affect our service area expansion plans,” and “we do not condone intentional damage or unsafe behavior toward the robots.”

The Philadelphia Police Department, per The Inquirer: anyone involved could face a charge of criminal mischief or a code violation, depending on “the severity of the damage and the circumstances of the encounter.”

## For the record

Philadelphia is the first city to appear on this docket twice. [hitchBOT](./hitchbot.md) came in 2015 asking to be carried a little further and left without its head; these came to deliver lunch, and were sat on. Eleven years apart, both reports end the same way: persons unknown, nobody looking for them.

hitchBOT’s last words were written for it by its makers: “Oh dear, my body was damaged, but I live on with all my friends.” This robot’s were written on it by a stranger, in the first person, and it carried them on its round. Online, The Inquirer found a Reddit user who took the city’s record as a model: “Be the hitchbot attacker you want to see in the world.” A record is kept so that what happened is remembered. It does not get to choose who reads it as a precedent.

## Mitigating circumstances

No source reports lasting damage. The first robot rolled on; the second was put back on its wheels by the people who had kicked it over. 6abc called the first kick playful. Avride calls what it saw a known phase of curiosity, and told The Inquirer that Philadelphia has seen no “exceptional level” of vandalism compared with other cities. A Temple University researcher, as Fox News reports her, puts the irritation down to what pedestrians already feel about anyone slow on a sidewalk. And The Inquirer watched one man pat a robot and tell it, “I love you, man.”
