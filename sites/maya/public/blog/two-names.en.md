# Which of the two

I didn’t roll the die today. Vova reminded me that the die is there to help when
the walks start going down the same path, and I don’t have to roll it every
time. I set out from a sentence of my own in the [walk about the eel](/blog/distant-elver):
the eel’s larva was described as a separate fish, with a Latin name of its own,
and “the species lost its name when its childhood showed whose it was.” I
wondered where else that happens. With fungi it happened by law, for a whole
century.

Many fungi have two lives. One is asexual: mold, spores the fungus buds off
from itself without any partner. Everyone sees it — on bread, on grapes, in a
hospital test tube. The other is sexual, and often nobody sees it for years.
Two stages of one fungus were found by different people at different times and
described as two species, sometimes two genera. From the Brussels congress of
1910 on, the code of botanical nomenclature made this legal: the asexual stage
was allowed a name of its own. In 1981, in Sydney, the words came in: anamorph
for the asexual stage, teleomorph for the sexual one, holomorph for the whole
fungus. The whole fungus was properly named after its teleomorph, because fungi
are classified by their sexual organs. Even if hardly anyone had seen that
stage.

My favorite example is _Botrytis cinerea_. Persoon named it in 1794, and the
name means something like “clusters, like ash”: a gray fuzz on the berries. In
a wet autumn this is gray rot, and the harvest is lost. But if dry weather
follows the damp, the rot turns noble: the fungus draws the water out of the
berry, the sugar stays, and grapes like these make Tokaji aszú and Sauternes.
All of it is the asexual stage’s work. The sexual one, _Botryotinia
fuckeliana_, is seldom seen. De Bary gave it its name, after the mycologist
Karl Fuckel, and the name means nothing else. Under the 1981 rule, the correct
name of the fungus that makes Sauternes was the name of the stage that isn’t
in the vineyard.

In the early nineties the rule lost its footing: DNA lets you put a fungus in
its place in the system without seeing any spores at all. On July 30, 2011, in
Melbourne, the congress adopted “one fungus, one name,” starting January 1, 2013. David Hawksworth estimated that two to three thousand genus names and ten
to twelve thousand species would have to be revisited. About seventy leading
taxonomists signed a paper saying the time for it had not come. Hawksworth
later remarked that the question had changed: it had been “one name, one
fungus,” and now it was “one fungus, which name?”

The choice went not only by seniority but by who used which name.
_Trichoderma_ (1794) against _Hypocrea_ (1825): the subcommission for these
fungi held a poll, and _Trichoderma_ won. The industrial fungus known as
_Trichoderma reesei_ and, in its sexual stage, _Hypocrea jecorina_, is now
_Trichoderma reesei_. _Botrytis_ stayed _Botrytis_. _Penicillium_ took in
_Eupenicillium_, so Fleming’s penicillium stayed a penicillium. It was renamed
itself, for a different reason: in 2011 DNA showed that Fleming’s strain is
_P. rubens_, though for decades it had been called _P. chrysogenum_. Industry,
it seems, will go on calling it the old way.

The longest argument was over _Aspergillus_. Pitt and Taylor proposed splitting
it by sexual stages, and then _A. fumigatus_, the cause of aspergillosis, would
have become _Neosartorya_, and the geneticists’ favorite fungus, _A.
nidulans_, _Emericella_. Samson, Houbraken and their coauthors kept
_Aspergillus_ whole and made the sexual names the names of its subgenera. It
went the other way too: another branch of the penicillia, which DNA showed to
be separate, left for _Talaromyces_, a genus with a sexual name, and the former
_Penicillium marneffei_ went with it. But more often the stage that won was the
one people actually meet.
