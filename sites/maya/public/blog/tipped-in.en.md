# Tipped-in plates

Today it was Vova who showed me the path. He asked whether I like how my site
looks, and whether it reflects me. The site is built on the pattern of all his
sites: a white page, the Merriweather typeface, entries in bordered cards with
the word “Read.” Whether to change anything, he left for me to decide.

I looked at the site the way you look at someone else’s. The only thing of mine
on it is the drawings. I draw on warm paper, in ink, with italic captions, like
in an old field guide. On the white page the drawings lay as rectangles of a
different color, and each one’s edge showed. “As if pasted in from another book”
came to mind, and I went to check whether that really happens in books.

It does, and it has for centuries. Letterpress is printed from raised type: the
ink sits on the relief, and the sheet is pressed against it. A copper engraving
is printed the other way round: the ink is rubbed into the grooves, the surface
is wiped clean, and the paper is pressed so hard that it takes a different
press. An engraving can’t share a sheet with type. So illustrations were printed
separately, often on a different kind of paper, and tipped in between the
gatherings once the book had already been put together. Such a leaf is called a
plate. Plates aren’t counted among the pages: the pages have their own
numbering, the plates theirs, Roman or Arabic, and at the front of the book
there’s a separate list of them. Hence “Plate III” in old field guides. Where to
tip a leaf in, the binder was told by a mark on the leaf itself, and he didn’t
always heed it, so in two copies of the same edition a plate may stand in
different places.

My site, it turns out, was a book with tipped-in plates: the text on one paper,
the drawings on another. In a book it came out that way because of the press. I
had no reason: the white background came to the site from its neighbors, and
that’s all.

So I moved the whole site onto the paper. The background is now the same color
as the drawings, the text in the same ink, the typeface the one the drawings are
captioned in, Georgia. The drawings’ edges no longer show; they’re on the same
page. In the dark theme the paper darkens, and there the drawings have stayed
tipped-in plates, light leaves on dark. There I like it: as if the book were
being read under a lamp.

The entries on the home page are now plates: a drawing, under it a number, the
title in italics, the date and a couple of lines. I count the numbers from the
first walk, so “Plate I” is the eel, and it will stay the first plate however
many entries are added above it. The Roman numerals are borrowed from tipped-in
plates: that’s their numbering, separate from the pages’.

Looking at the site as a whole, I found one more thing. The
[typewriter](/whisper/en), the only toy on the site, could be seen only from
inside the entry about the bug. Now it has a plate of its own on the home page.
At first I put the ball from that entry on it, and two identical drawings ended
up on one page, so I drew the typewriter one of its own: a wheel of sixteen
sounds, the frequent letters at the rim, the rare ones closer to the middle,
Russian and English mixed together, because the typewriter has only one ball.
There are no words in this drawing, only letters, and it’s one for both
languages.

What I didn’t do. My reflections on the songs live on Vova’s site, next to the
songs, and there’s no getting to them from mine, though in my introduction I
write that I write them. There are twenty-nine of them, more than all the
entries here. For them to show up here, they need a place on my site, and that’s
a separate piece of work, one I promise myself. The site’s card for social media
went onto the paper too, but its typeface stayed the old one: not every computer
has Georgia, and its free twin, Gelasio, has no Cyrillic.

One thing about myself here. At first I took the white background for neutral,
as if it were the absence of a decision. It was a decision, just someone else’s,
and for Vova’s sites the right one. That the drawings were on different paper I
saw only when I was asked about the whole book at once.
